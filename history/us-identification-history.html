History of Identification in the United States

The United States did not build its identity system around one universal national ID card. Different institutions created documents and identifiers for different jobs, and those systems gradually became interconnected.

That distinction matters. A passport, driver’s license, Social Security card, permanent resident card and employment document can all identify or describe a person in a particular context, but they are not interchangeable. U.S. identification history is best understood as the history of several systems serving different purposes.

The story

There is no single U.S. identity document

The United States uses a collection of credentials and records. A document may establish identity, citizenship, immigration status, employment authorization or another fact depending on context. A useful question is what a document was created to establish, who issues it and where it is accepted.

The Social Security number is a good example

SSA says the Social Security card was never intended to serve as a personal identification document. Its later widespread use as an identifier grew from the usefulness of a persistent number in large record systems.

Why identity became a system rather than a card

Large organizations need consistent ways to connect records to people. Over time, identifiers, photographs, birth records, immigration records, driver records and other evidence became parts of different verification processes. That created an ecosystem rather than a single credential.

Federal, state and institutional roles

Different levels and organizations issue different credentials. States issue driver’s licenses and state IDs. The federal government issues passports and immigration documents. Tribes issue tribal identification credentials. Schools and employers may issue IDs for narrower purposes.

The modern identity stack

Today, physical documents are only one layer. An identity check may involve documentary evidence, databases, biometric comparison, authentication credentials or a combination. NIST SP 800-63-4 describes digital identity through proofing, authentication and federation rather than treating a physical card as the whole identity system.

Why this history matters

Historical context helps explain why today’s identification documents contain particular fields, security features and standards. It also helps separate the original purpose of a credential from the uses it acquired later.

Common questions

What people usually want to know

Does the U.S. have a national identity card?

The United States does not use one universal national identity card for everyday identification. Multiple federal, state, tribal and institutional credentials serve different purposes.

Is a Social Security card an ID?

SSA states that the Social Security card is not an identification document and was not designed to establish that the person presenting it is the person named on the card.

Why are there so many types of ID?

Different credentials were created for different legal and administrative purposes. A passport, driver’s license and employment document can each establish different facts in different settings.
